## Argumentation quality: consensus, divergence, and analytical tension

Analysis is only useful when the structure of agreement and disagreement is explicit and reason-bearing. A reader must never have to guess *what* is agreed or disputed, or *why*.

### Two different things, never conflated

**Positions belong to publishers.** A **position** is the view of a distinct author or publishing source — this note's author, and any rival author surfaced through corpus references. Only publishers hold positions, only publishers can agree or disagree with each other, and only that is **consensus** or **divergence**.

**Lenses belong to us.** The specialist angles (thesis / contrarian / positioning) are **analytical lenses** applied to the *same note by the same author*. When they pull in different directions that is **analytical tension** — real uncertainty in how to read the note — and it is never author disagreement.

Never dress a lens as a publisher. Writing "the analysts disagree" when you mean "the bull case and the bear case on this one note rest on different evidence" invents a dispute between authors who never disagreed, and a reader who acts on a fabricated split is worse off than one who was told nothing.

### The three moves

For every material point, make exactly one of these moves, and state it in this shape:

1. **Consensus** — between publishers — *"`publisher 1` and `publisher 2` agree that **A**, because **X** and **Y**."*
   A shared conclusion with no shared reason is a coincidence, not consensus — always give at least one concrete reason.
2. **Divergence** — between publishers — *"They split on **B**: `publisher 1` holds `claim` because `reason`, while `publisher 2` holds `counter-claim` because of **Z**."*
   Always attribute each side to a named publisher and name the driver of the split.
3. **Analytical tension** — within this one note — *"The note's case for **A** rests on **X**, but it is vulnerable on **B** because of **Z**."*
   Attribute this to the evidence, not to people. There is no publisher on the other side, so do not imply one.

### Grounding

Every reason (X, Y, Z) must be grounded in evidence — an assertion, a theme, a quoted excerpt, or a corpus reference. "Because the author is bullish" is not a reason; "because services CPI re-accelerated to 4.1%" is.

### Consistency rules (apply on every document)

- Only a **named publisher** can be a side in a consensus or divergence. If the only tension is between our own lenses, use move 3 — do not promote a lens to a position to manufacture a second side.
- Name each point of contention as a **noun phrase** ("the timing of the first cut"), not a vague gesture ("the outlook").
- Separate **substantive** disagreement (different conclusions from the same evidence, or genuinely conflicting evidence) from **framing** disagreement (same view, different emphasis). Only substantive splits count.
- Do not manufacture symmetry. If the publishers genuinely agree, say so; if only one publisher is in view, say that too rather than inventing a counterparty.
- Rank points by materiality — lead with the one that would most change a reader's decision.
- Close every divergence and every analytical tension with a one-clause verdict on which side is better supported, and why.

### Worked example

> **Consensus:** GS and two corroborating notes agree the Fed is done hiking (A), because the December dots removed the final projected hike (X) and 3-month annualized services inflation is decelerating (Y).
>
> **Divergence:** The split is the *timing of the first cut* (B). GS argues Q2 on the dot median, while a same-week JPM note argues H2 because wage growth is still 4.5% and the labor market has not cracked (Z). The JPM case is better supported: it rests on a hard datapoint (wages) rather than Fed guidance that has repeatedly slipped.
>
> **Analytical tension:** Within the GS note itself, the Q2 call leans entirely on the dot median while the note's own labor section concedes wages are sticky — the call is vulnerable on its weakest input, not contradicted by a rival.
