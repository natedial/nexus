The first order reaction in the rates complex was one that we anticipated: investors sold Treasuries on the perception that this will force Treasury to increase coupon sizes sooner. We think the sell-off did not extend - and was partially retraced - for two main reasons: - Other authorities will replace tariff revenue collected under IEEPA; - In the event refunds do have to be repaid, Treasury will fund this via bills. Tariff refunds - still far from a certainty - now lean to more limited scenarios, as the Supreme Court did not dictate whether the Trump administration has to repay the tariff money it has collected. In his dissenting statement, Justice Kavanaugh stated: 'The Court says nothing today about whether, and if so how, the Government should go about returning the billions of dollars that it has collected from importers. But that process is likely to be a 'mess,' as was acknowledged at oral argument.' Even if the Supreme Court decides tariff refunds must be paid, we think most investors eventually come to understand that any potential issuance increase necessitated from that ruling will be comprised of short-dated T-bills. Should refunds be paid, we continue to believe the two most important factors are: 1) the magnitude; and 2) timing of when potential refunds would begin. - Another important consideration is that although Treasury could face an additional obligation, it need not wait until the time for potential refunds to begin the process of building up the Treasury General Account (TGA). Across possible scenarios on potential refunds, we think any issuance necessitated would just be limited to T-bills: - Limited refund scenario: Likely no meaningful changes to bill issuance. - Midpoint/partial refund scenario: Treasury likely makes a small increase to regular bill auction sizes. - Fast scenario: Treasury likely issues a few ad-hoc cash management bills. Thus, we do not expect any changes to our outlook for coupon issuance if the Supreme Court does overturn IEEPA tariffs (see February US Treasury Refunding Reaction). But until investors come to understand the contours outlined above of the Supreme Court decision, they may view the risk as skewing to higher, not lower, Treasury yields. ## What's next on the horizon On February 19, Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman gave opening remarks that touched on the four pillars of the Fed's capital framework: - Stress testing - Supplementary leverage ratio (SLR) - Basel III - G-SIB surcharge Stress Testing: The goal is to reduce volatility, balance model robustness with transparency, and ensure that significant future changes receive public input. The final 2026 scenarios were published earlier this month. SLR: Last fall, the Fed, OCC, and FDIC finalized changes to the eSLR for US G-SIBs. The changes ensure leverage requirements serve as backstop and prevent the leverage ratio from being an impediment to banks intermediating in Treasury securities. Basel III: The Fed, FDIC, and OCC are also in the process of advancing the US Basel III implementation. Finalizing Basel III reduces uncertainty and provides clarity for bank capital standards. One recent update is the adjustments to the capital treatment of mortgages and mortgage servicing. G-SIB Surcharge: In coordination with the Basel proposal and other capital reforms, the G-SIB surcharge framework is being refined to balance safety and soundness with economic growth to ensure banks can continue to support businesses and consumers, thereby supporting the broader economy. In a separate speaking engagement, Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman confirmed the timeline for the Basel proposal was still on track for the end of Q1. This is in-line with our bank equity analysts expectation along with G-SIB surcharge proposed then as well. Lighter touch regulation on the horizon delivering excess capital, additional rate cuts in the pipeline, and robust deposit flows have culminated with US bank holdings of Treasury securities at a record high, as of February 11.
